Rephrase Before You Act

Characterizing and Mitigating Language Sensitivity in Vision-Language-Action Models

Anonymous authors · under review · paper (PDF)

Abstract

Vision-language-action models (VLAs) are strikingly sensitive to instruction phrasing and do not inherit the language robustness of the vision-language models they are built on. A one-word edit can move success by tens of points: π0.5 turns on a LIBERO stove 100% of the time for “switch on the stove” and 2% for “switch on the hot plate”, and a π0 checkpoint finetuned with rephrase augmentation still shows swings of up to 61 points. We characterize this sensitivity with statistically tested single-edit swings and an oracle phrase search, which shows that phrasing alone nearly closes the 21-point gap between in-distribution and out-of-distribution tasks. We then reduce it without modifying the policy. Because the sensitivity is systematic, it can be expressed as explicit rules: we score many phrasings of a few training tasks, have a large language model distill the evidence into ten to twenty rephrasing rules, and at deployment rewrite each incoming instruction once under these rules. The rules improve the frozen π0 by 16 to 27% relative on twelve held-out tasks across adversarial, VLM-generated, and human-generated phrasings, with gains concentrated on out-of-distribution tasks. The pipeline replicates on π0.5 and LIBERO, lifting in-finetune success from 93.6% to 97.8%. The method requires no retraining and no per-step verification, and applies zero-shot to unseen tasks and instructions.

Language sensitivity of vision-language-action models

A vision-language-action model (VLA) is a robot controller. It looks at a camera image, reads a short written instruction such as “put the carrot on the plate”, and moves the robot arm. VLAs are built on top of vision-language models, the same kind of model behind chat assistants that can look at pictures, so you might expect them to understand an instruction however it is worded. Often they do not. Changing one word, without changing the meaning, can turn a task the robot almost always gets right into one it almost always gets wrong. Three examples, measured on the same scenes with the same random seeds, so the wording is the only difference:

69% “purple eggplant goes on the sponge”  vs.  8% “eggplant goes on the sponge”

69% “put the pepsi on the plate”  vs.  25% “put the Pepsi on the plate”

100% “fire up the stove”  vs.  6% “turn on the stove”

Throughout, a task is one thing the robot is asked to do in one scene, and the success rate is the share of attempts in which it finished. We study two recent VLAs, π0 and π0.5, on two standard simulated test beds of tabletop tasks, Bridge and LIBERO.

We measure the sensitivity in two ways: single-edit swings, pairs of instructions that differ by one edit, and oracles, the best instruction we can find for each task.

Single-edit swings

A single-edit swing is a pair of instructions that differ by a single edit, one word or even one capital letter, where the success rate changes by more than chance can explain. Oracles

The second measurement: for each task we search for the best wording we can find, which we call the task’s oracle, and compare it with the other ways the task can be worded. The search is simple. A vision-language model proposes 16 rephrasings, the robot tries each one many times, and the next round of proposals sees the scores so far; after three rounds we keep the best. The oracle is then re-measured on fresh attempts so that luck in the search does not inflate it.

The instruction groups we compare, which come up again in the results:

  • Canonical (or original): the instruction that comes with the task. For tasks in the training data, this is the wording the robot was trained on.
  • Natural: rephrasings worded the way a person might ask. Two sources: written by a vision-language model (VLM naturals), or written by people in our survey (human naturals, described below).
  • Adversarial: deliberately convoluted, over-complicated wordings, written by a vision-language model.
  • Oracle: the best wording found by the search above.

Mitigating language sensitivity

A common and effective fix is rephrase augmentation: during training, each demonstration is labeled with several different wordings, so the model sees more variety. The π0 we study was trained this way, and it helps, about 2 points on human-written instructions in our measurement. But as the examples above show, large swings remain. We build on top of that fix with a method that changes nothing inside the robot’s model; it is explained in the next section. Our method

Our key observation is that the sensitivity is systematic rather than random. The same kinds of wording help or hurt across many tasks, so the pattern can be written down as rules. The method has three steps:

  1. Gather evidence. Take a handful of training tasks. A vision-language model writes many rephrasings of each, the robot tries every one, and we record how often each wording works.
  2. Distill rules. A large language model reads all of that evidence and writes 10 to 20 plain-English rephrasing rules, for example “‘coke’ stays ‘coke’, never ‘cola’, ‘soda’, or bare ‘can’” and “never swap a common noun for its sibling: keep ‘plate’, not ‘dish’”. This happens once, ahead of time.
  3. Apply the rules. When a new instruction arrives, a vision-language model rewrites it once, following the rules, and the rewrite goes to the robot.

The robot’s model is never changed, nothing runs while the robot moves, and because the rules describe how the robot reads language in general, they carry over to tasks it has never seen.

Human-written instructions

To test on instructions that real people would actually write, we ran a survey. Each respondent watched a short looping video of the robot doing a task and typed how they would ask for it, once as if asking an 8-year-old, once an adult, and once a robot. They never saw the original instruction. 37 people aged 11 to 85 took part, with familiarity with AI ranging from none to professional. After dropping a few blank or incomplete entries and duplicates, this gave 363 unique instructions, kept exactly as typed, typos included. Below is one task as respondents saw it.

Results and experiments

We test on π0 with 12 held-out Bridge tasks that contributed no evidence, 24 scenes each. Three different models serve as the rewriter (the paper calls it the applier): Claude Fable 5, Gemini (the gemini-pro-latest model as of September 2026), and Qwen3.5-9B, an open-weights model that runs on a local GPU.

The rules come in three kinds of rulebook, which differ only in the evidence the rule-writer saw:

  • In-finetune: rephrasings of tasks from the robot’s own training data, scored without running the robot, by how closely its planned motion matched the human demonstration, together with the training instructions themselves.
  • Out-of-finetune: rephrasings of eight simulated tasks the robot was never trained on, scored by actually running the robot.
  • Both: all of the above.

Each kind was distilled three times and the results averaged. Every rewriter and rulebook is tested on the four instruction groups defined above: adversarial, VLM-written natural, human-written natural, and canonical.

What we found. Rulebooks built from out-of-finetune evidence are the best kind on every group except canonical. They improve the unmodified robot by 16 to 27% relative on the adversarial and natural groups, depending on the rewriter, and most of that gain comes on out-of-distribution tasks. The result is stable across the three rewriters and three independent distillations. The same pipeline, repeated on π0.5 and LIBERO, lifts success on trained tasks from 93.6% to 97.8%.

Conclusions

  • VLAs are very sensitive to wording, and the sensitivity is systematic. On π0 the best wording nearly closes the 21-point gap between in-distribution and out-of-distribution tasks, so much of the apparent generalization gap is a wording gap.
  • It can be reduced without touching the robot’s model. Evidence from a few training tasks, distilled by a language model into 10 to 20 explicit rules, improves a frozen π0 by 16 to 27% relative on held-out tasks, with the gains concentrated out-of-distribution.
  • The method is cheap and general: one rewrite per instruction, no retraining, no checking during the motion, transfer to unseen tasks, and a replication on π0.5 and LIBERO.
